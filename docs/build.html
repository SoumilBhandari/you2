01 / The object

A wedge with one round eye, sized by its parts

A 22° raked face carries an 87.6 mm round display; the vertical skirt below it carries a forward-firing driver; four microphones sit on the top surface as far from that driver as the box allows. Everything vents out the back, because a vent on the front or top is a leak path from the speaker straight into the microphones.

One number in the model is purely about how it looks — fp_r, the corner radius shared by the faceplate and its rebate. At 6 mm the face was a hard-cornered rectangle carrying a circle inside the shell's own rectangle; at 16 it reads as one soft shape around the eye, and the four exposed screws sit on the curve. 17 leaves under three perimeters of material outboard of a screw counterbore, and the model refuses it.

Almost nothing about the size was chosen. The display carrier sets the face length, the driver box sets the skirt, the mic array sets the depth, and the fastener flange sets the width — the model derives the rest and refuses to render if a measurement makes it unbuildable.

Three gates run over this model, and they all pass. cad/check.py proves every part is a single connected solid; cad/fit.py proves no two parts occupy the same space; and twenty-one asserts inside the model itself check the things neither can see — that the display posts compress their gasket by 20 % rather than floating above it, that the driver clamp's webs clear the driver box, that the microphone array lands on the top face instead of overhanging its front edge, and that every plate still fits the bed. Between them they caught twenty-three defects that renders did not show — including two Ø10 × 24 mm horizontal cantilevers that would have needed support towers built from the bare bed inside a sealed cavity, and a base-plate boss whose insert bore had no floor because the microSD window ran underneath it.

02 / Before you buy anything

Seven numbers, and one of them will be wrong

Every dimension in the model came from a datasheet. Vendor listings for these parts are routinely wrong, and the enclosure is derived from them — so a bad number does not cause a tolerance problem, it causes a reprint of a 392 g part.

ParameterAssumedWhy it matters
mic_circle_d44.0 mmCheck this before you buy anything else. Seeed describes the array as “44 mm spacing arranged in a circular layout”. If that means the pitch-circle diameter, the design works. If it means the distance between adjacent capsules — which is what “spacing” means on the linear-4 sibling — then the pitch circle is 44 × √2 = 62.2 mm, and at that value the model refuses to render. Precisely: the ports do not fall off the board — the counterbore's outer edge sits 2.3 mm inside a Ø73 PCB — but that breaks the model's 4 mm edge keep-out by 1.7 mm, and 2.3 mm of rim is not enough to bond a Ø6 membrane onto. That is not a tolerance problem, it is a different mic layout. Resolve it with calipers or an email before anything else.
disp_carrier115.0 mmSets the raked face length, which is 107 mm of the 190 mm height.
disp_panel_d / disp_active91.5 / 87.6Two different numbers, and vendors quote whichever flatters. The bezel overlap (panel − aperture)/2 is the only thing retaining the glass; if the panel is smaller than assumed, that goes to zero.
disp_hole_p105.0 mmThe carrier's own mounting-hole pitch. The display bolts straight to the faceplate on these.
spk_bx/by/bz50 × 45 × 22The driver's height sets the 65 mm skirt. A shorter driver is the cheapest way to shrink the whole object.
spk_cone_d36 mmThe radiating diameter, not the box. Grille holes outside it are blocked and do nothing.
mic_pcb_d73.0 mmWith the rake, this sets the depth. 150 mm currently leaves 13 mm spare.
Print the faceplate first. It is 55 g and about 5 hours, and it is the only part that touches the display. Four of the seven numbers above are confirmed or destroyed the moment you offer the real panel up to it — for a fifteenth of the filament the shell would cost.

When you have measured, edit the parameters at the top of cad/you2-revD.scad and run the gate. It checks that every part is still a single connected solid, which is the specific defect that made the previous revision unbuildable:

python3 cad/check.py cad/you2-revD.scad

Ballast, if you want it. The two pods hold 63 cm³ each. Dry sand is ~101 g apiece, steel shot ~283 g. They use volume that is otherwise dead, cost no height, and put the mass at the very bottom — which matters because the real cable-tug failure is the device sliding, and slide force scales directly with weight. They are genuinely optional; skip plate 4 and nothing else changes.
Two-tone for free. Print the faceplate in black and the shell in a light colour — or the reverse. It costs nothing but a filament change between plates and it does more for how the object reads than any geometry change on this page.

04 / The six print plates

Every plate fits a 220 × 220 bed, and none of them need support

The shell is the whole trick: printed top face down, the bottom opening faces up so the cavity has no ceiling, the raked face and skirt become steep self-supporting walls, and the four microphone ports print crisp against the plate. Print it any other way and you bury support inside a sealed box.

Bed contact is 141.6 cm², and the part widens as it rises — the top face is 152 × 92 mm and the full section is 152 × 150 mm by two thirds height. Use a brim.

What "no supports" actually means here. I measured it rather than asserting it: measured in the model's own plate-1 orientation, 141.6 cm² of the shell is bed contact and 31.2 cm² faces downward at more than 60°. The largest single contribution is 7.2 cm² of 1.2 mm shadow-gap ledge, which any slicer bridges; the next largest patch is 2.7 cm², only seven of the 143 patches exceed 1 cm² at all, and 6.6 cm² of the total falls in the first 20 mm of the print. There is no large flat unsupported span anywhere — which is the thing that actually fails. An earlier version had two horizontal hoop ribs contributing 32 cm² of unsupported ledge on their own; they are now vertical fins, which in this orientation overhang nothing at all.
The shell is a ~30 hour print at 0.2 mm. That is one power cut or one filament runout away from a wasted 392 g. Use a 0.6 mm nozzle at 0.28 mm and it drops to about 15 hours with no meaningful loss — nothing on this part needs 0.4 mm resolution except the grille, and the grille holes are 2.8 mm.
TPU is optional. Plate 5 is 8 g and prints fine on a Bowden machine if you slow it down. Plate 6 — the foot — is the only substantial flexible print, and four stick-on silicone bumpers do the same job if you would rather not run TPU at all.

08 / Tools

Five things

  • Soldering iron with a conical or heat-set tip — For the 24 inserts. ~200 °C. This is the only 'soldering' in the build.
  • Hex drivers, 2.0 mm and 2.5 mm — M3 and M2.5 socket caps.
  • Small Phillips / PZ1 — M2.5 pan heads.
  • Digital calipers — Non-negotiable. Seven dimensions have to be checked before you print.
  • Tweezers, isopropyl, a craft knife — Membranes, gasket seating, cleaning the bed.

09 / Assembly

Twelve steps, in this order

The order matters in one specific way: the display never enters the shell. It bolts to the faceplate, outside, and the faceplate goes on last. That is the whole reason the face is a separate part.

  1. Install the 24 heat-set insertsIron at ~200 °C, straight down, let each cool before loading. Every hole is bored smaller than the insert's own diameter — Ø4.0 for a 4.6 mm M3, Ø3.6 for a 4.0 mm M3, Ø3.2 for a 3.5 mm M2.5. Bore at the insert diameter and it spins out the first time you torque a screw. Orientation matters: the two mic inserts face downward with the shell upright, so do those with the shell inverted, and use a stubby iron — they sit 184 mm down a blind well.
  2. Fit the acoustic membranesOne self-adhesive ePTFE disc into each of the four counterbores on the outside of the top face. This is what makes the top face survive a spill without closing the acoustic path. Glue a scrap of mesh fabric behind the speaker grille from the inside.
  3. Ballast pods (optional)Fill each with dry sand to ~3 mm below the rim, press the lid in, run a bead of superglue round the seam. ~110 g each. Set aside.
  4. Base: Pi and podsFour M2.5 standoffs into the base inserts. Pi on top, long axis front-to-back, USB-A and Ethernet facing the rear, four M2.5 × 6. Check the microSD lines up with the service slot before going further. Screw the pods down either side.
  5. DriverThe TPU speaker gasket onto the raised seat in the skirt, driver onto the gasket, clamp bar over it, two M3 × 10. Snug, not tight — the gasket is the mount, and crushing it defeats the decoupling. Route the JST lead up the side away from any other cable.
  6. Mic arrayGasket onto the ceiling, array up against it, clamp ring over the PCB rim, two M3 × 8. Tighten until the screws bottom on the bosses — they are hard stops, so the gasket compresses exactly 0.3 mm and cannot be crushed. Check each capsule sits under its port before you tighten.
  7. reSpeaker core boardCable-tie to a side rib. USB to the Pi.
  8. Power cableGrommet into the rear hole, PSU plug through it from outside, into the cable clamp, then into the Pi's USB-C. Tighten the clamp on the cable jacket, never on the connector.
  9. Display onto the faceplatePanel gasket into the aperture, panel down onto it, carrier onto the four posts, four M2.5 × 6. The bezel overlaps the panel border — that overlap is the entire retention. If the panel is loose, stop and re-measure disp_panel_d.
  10. Connect the display, then close the faceConnect the FFC to the Pi's DSI-2 connector with the shell and faceplate side by side. Close the ZIF latch and put a dab of hot glue on it — a ZIF has no retention against vibration. Fold the slack, never coil it, then seat the faceplate into its rebate and fit four M3 × 10 socket caps.
  11. Base onto the shellFour M3 × 10 countersunk, up into the corner bosses.
  12. FootPush the TPU shoe on, or stick four silicone bumpers to the base. It should stand 3 mm proud.

10 / Wiring

In the default build there is nothing to solder

Everything is USB, a DSI ribbon, a JST plug and screws. The optional light bar is the only soldering in the entire project, and it is the reason the default has none.

  • One mains plug. The official 27 W PSU's captive cable passes through a rubber grommet in the rear face, through an internal clamp, and into the Pi's USB-C. No panel-mount coupler — that would put two more contact interfaces in series with a 5 A rail feeding a board that is fussy about supply voltage.
  • Audio stays on USB. Playback and capture must be the same UAC2 device or the echo canceller has no sample-aligned reference. This is most of why the reSpeaker is the pick.
  • The speaker output is bridged. Both leads swing. Neither may be grounded and neither may be used as a reference for anything. Verify against your exact SKU — grounding one destroys the amplifier.
  • Feed the display 5 V separately from the Pi header. The DSI ribbon cannot supply enough current, and skipping this gives a display that half-works. Run the 5 V and its return as a twisted pair alongside the ribbon so the loop area stays small.
  • Strain relief everywhere. Cable clamp on the mains jacket. Hot glue on the DSI ZIF latch. Cable tie on the reSpeaker lead. Every connector in here is a surface-mount part on a board you cannot easily replace.

12 / What is still unknown

Nothing here has been built

The geometry is gated — every part exports as one connected solid, every plate fits the bed, and the model asserts its own buildability. None of that is the same as having made one.

  • Barge-in. Whether the echo canceller holds with a driver this close to the array. Test it on a bench with the parts loose, before any of this exists — long response at normal volume, wake word over the top from 2 m and 4 m. If it fails, nothing else on this page matters.
  • Which speaker, exactly. The part has no number and no target playback level is written down anywhere, so the echo budget cannot be computed before ordering.
  • Whether the reSpeaker is 16 kHz-capped over USB. If it is, the four-mic upgrade buys beamforming rather than bandwidth, and voice quality is capped whatever you pick.
  • Real interior temperature at the 60 mm rear clearance the venting needs — and what happens when someone pushes it against a wall, at which point the box is sealed.
  • Whether mic_circle_d is 44. The cheapest thing in the project to check and the most expensive to get wrong.
